Quick Answer

Mornings before school can be hard because anticipatory anxiety begins before the child even enters the classroom. A child may wake up already thinking about greetings, attendance, questions, peers, teachers, transitions, lunch, group work, or being expected to speak. By the time they reach school, their nervous system may already be highly activated.

This can show up as silence, stomachaches, irritability, slow getting ready, refusal, tears, clinging, arguing, or seeming “fine” until the last minute. The problem is not always the morning routine itself. The morning may be when the child’s brain starts preparing for a day full of communication demands.

Research Explanation

Selective mutism is strongly connected to anxiety, and anxiety often begins before the feared situation. This is called anticipatory anxiety. The child is not only reacting to school once they arrive. They may be reacting to what they expect school to require from them.

School contains many possible speaking demands. A child may be expected to say hello, answer attendance, ask for help, speak to peers, respond to teachers, participate in groups, order lunch, read aloud, or explain needs. For a child with selective mutism, these expectations can feel threatening even before they happen.

Anticipatory anxiety can create a buildup effect. The child’s body may become tense, alert, and stressed during breakfast, while getting dressed, in the car, or at the school entrance. Parents may see resistance and assume the child is trying to avoid school. Sometimes that is partly true, but the deeper issue is that school feels overwhelming.

This buildup can also affect behavior. Anxiety may appear as irritability, refusal, rushing, freezing, crying, or arguing. A child may not be able to explain, “I am scared that someone will ask me a question today.” Instead, they may say their stomach hurts, refuse shoes, complain about clothes, or become silent.

Research on selective mutism emphasizes that treatment often needs to happen in the places where speech is difficult, especially school. Oerbeck and colleagues developed home- and school-based interventions because school is often where symptoms are most visible and most impairing. Follow-up work suggests that school-based, exposure-focused treatment can support meaningful improvement over time. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

A key insight for parents is that mornings are not just logistics. They are emotional transition periods. The child is moving from a safe environment to a higher-demand environment. If that transition is unpredictable, rushed, or full of reminders to speak, anxiety can increase before the day begins.

Another important concept is “reducing decision load.” An anxious brain has less room for flexible decision-making. If mornings require too many choices, instructions, corrections, and transitions, the child may become overwhelmed before school even starts.

This is why predictable morning scripts, visual routines, packed bags, simple clothing decisions, and calm transitions can be helpful. These strategies do not treat selective mutism by themselves, but they reduce the amount of stress the child carries into school.

For some children, a planned low-pressure school entry routine can also help. Instead of walking into a chaotic hallway and immediately facing social demands, the child might enter a few minutes early, walk with a trusted adult, deliver a note, feed a class pet, organize materials, or begin with a quiet task. The goal is to let the nervous system settle before communication demands begin.

What This Means For Parents

If mornings are difficult, try to look for the anxiety underneath the behavior. Ask yourself: What is my child anticipating? What part of the school day might feel hardest? Is there a specific transition, teacher, peer, greeting, or speaking demand that starts the anxiety cycle?

A helpful strategy is to make mornings boring in the best possible way. Use the same order, the same phrases, the same materials, and fewer last-minute decisions. Predictability lowers uncertainty.

You can also move problem-solving out of the morning. The morning is usually not the best time to lecture, negotiate, or plan speech goals. Those conversations are easier after school or during a calm evening moment.

Before school, focus on regulation and transition. Use short phrases like “First shoes, then breakfast,” “We have the plan,” or “You can start with your quiet job.” Keep the tone calm and avoid repeated reminders about talking.

If possible, collaborate with the school on a soft-start routine. This might include entering through a quieter door, greeting with a wave instead of speech, starting with a classroom job, or having the teacher use comments rather than questions at arrival.

The goal is not to remove all school expectations. The goal is to help your child arrive with enough regulation to access communication later in the day.

What To Avoid

  • Avoid starting the day with reminders like “Make sure you talk today.”
  • Avoid turning the morning into a lecture about bravery or effort.
  • Avoid adding unnecessary choices and last-minute decisions.
  • Avoid assuming stomachaches or refusal are fake without considering anxiety.
  • Avoid rushing if rushing reliably increases shutdown.
  • Avoid discussing speech goals at the school entrance.
  • Avoid making arrival the first major speaking challenge of the day.
