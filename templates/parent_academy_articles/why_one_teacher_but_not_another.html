Quick Answer

A child with selective mutism may speak to one teacher but not another because each teacher can feel very different to the child’s nervous system. The difference is not always about which teacher is “nicer.” It may involve predictability, voice tone, classroom structure, public attention, wait time, pressure, relationship history, and whether the child has already had successful speaking experiences with that teacher.

A teacher who is warm but unpredictable may still feel difficult to speak to. A quieter teacher who gives choices, avoids surprise questions, and allows extra response time may feel safer. For children with selective mutism, small differences in adult behavior can have a large effect on whether speech feels possible.

Research Explanation

Selective mutism is highly context-dependent. The same child may speak in one classroom and remain silent in another, even when both teachers are kind and supportive. This happens because anxiety is influenced by more than general friendliness. It is shaped by the child’s perception of safety, control, predictability, and social risk.

Research on anxiety shows that uncertainty can increase threat perception. Children with selective mutism often do better when expectations are clear and routines are predictable. A teacher who asks sudden questions, changes routines often, or calls attention to speaking attempts may unintentionally increase anxiety, even if their intentions are positive.

Social evaluation is another major factor. Speaking to a teacher privately is different from speaking when classmates are listening. A child may be able to whisper to a teacher at the desk but freeze when asked the same question during circle time. The speaking task has not changed, but the social audience has.

Past learning also matters. If a child has successfully spoken to one teacher several times without embarrassment or pressure, the brain begins to associate that teacher with safer communication. If another teacher has only interacted with the child during high-pressure moments, the child may associate that teacher with silence, even if the teacher is caring.

This is why some children develop “speaking relationships” with one adult and “silent relationships” with another. Once silence becomes the expected pattern with a specific teacher, it can become harder to break. Not because the child refuses, but because the nervous system has learned that silence is the safest response in that relationship.

School-based interventions for selective mutism often use gradual exposure, stimulus fading, and planned communication steps. These approaches do not depend on simply telling the child to speak. Instead, they carefully manipulate the environment so speech can occur in manageable steps.

One of the most useful parent insights is to study the teacher your child can speak to. What is that teacher doing differently? It may be something very small: greeting the child privately, using a calm tone, avoiding direct eye contact during early speech attempts, giving choices instead of open-ended questions, waiting longer, or not making a big deal when the child speaks.

Once those safety signals are identified, they can sometimes be copied by the teacher your child does not yet speak to. This turns the question from “Why won’t my child talk to this teacher?” into “What conditions helped speech happen with the other teacher, and how can we recreate them?”

What This Means For Parents

If your child speaks to one teacher but not another, try not to frame it as a personality conflict. It may be about subtle differences in anxiety cues. The goal is not to blame either teacher. The goal is to understand what makes one setting feel safer.

A practical next step is to compare the two teacher relationships. Does one teacher ask fewer direct questions? Does one teacher give more time? Does one teacher interact during play instead of performance? Does one teacher avoid public praise? Does one teacher use routines that help your child know what to expect?

You can also ask the speaking teacher what worked. Sometimes teachers discover useful details that parents would never know. Maybe the child first spoke while handing in papers, during a one-on-one game, while another child was absent, or during a quiet cleanup routine. These details can become the blueprint for the next step.

A useful strategy is to create a low-pressure communication ladder with the quieter teacher. The first step might not be speech. It might be pointing, choosing, handing over a card, whispering to a parent nearby, or answering a yes/no question privately. Over time, the ladder can move toward direct speech.

The key is that the teacher should become associated with successful communication, not pressure. Small wins matter because they teach the child’s brain that communication with this teacher is safe.

What To Avoid

  • Avoid saying, “You talk to your other teacher, so you can talk to this one.”
  • Avoid assuming the silent teacher-child relationship means the teacher did something wrong.
  • Avoid asking teachers to force public participation.
  • Avoid surprise speaking demands in front of classmates.
  • Avoid over-celebrating speech in a way that makes the child feel watched.
  • Avoid expecting speech to transfer automatically from one teacher to another.
