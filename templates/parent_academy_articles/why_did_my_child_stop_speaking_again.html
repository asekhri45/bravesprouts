Quick Answer

It is common for progress in selective mutism to move up and down. A child may begin speaking more, then suddenly speak less after a school break, illness, new teacher, schedule change, family stress, social challenge, or unfamiliar environment. This does not always mean progress is gone.

A helpful way to understand setbacks is that the child may not have lost the skill. They may have temporarily lost access to the skill because anxiety increased. The words, confidence, and communication ability may still be there, but the nervous system may need easier steps before speech becomes available again.

Research Explanation

Selective mutism treatment and recovery are rarely perfectly linear. Parents often hope progress will look like a steady climb: silence, then whispering, then speech, then normal communication everywhere. In reality, improvement often includes plateaus, dips, and regressions.

Long-term follow-up studies and clinical treatment literature suggest that many children improve significantly with support, but progress can vary across time and settings. A child may make gains in one classroom, with one teacher, or during one routine, then struggle when the context changes.

This happens because selective mutism is highly context-dependent. Speech is not only a skill. It is also tied to the child’s perception of safety. When the environment changes, the child’s anxiety level can change too.

Common setback triggers include school transitions, vacations, summer break, a new teacher, a substitute teacher, illness, poor sleep, increased academic demands, friendship changes, family stress, holidays, crowded events, or changes in routine. Even positive events can create setbacks if they increase uncertainty.

One of the most important insights for parents is the difference between losing a skill and losing access to a skill. A child who previously whispered to a teacher or answered a peer may still have that ability. But if anxiety rises, the child may not be able to access that ability in the moment.

This is similar to how adults may perform well in familiar situations but struggle under stress. A person may know exactly what to say during practice but freeze during a public presentation. The knowledge is not gone. Stress blocks access.

Avoidance learning can also return during stressful periods. If a child becomes silent again and adults quickly speak for them, remove demands, or stop practice entirely, the nervous system may relearn that silence is the safest route. This does not mean parents should force speech. It means setbacks need gentle structure, not panic.

Evidence-aligned approaches usually respond to setbacks by lowering the difficulty temporarily and rebuilding success. Instead of demanding the child return immediately to their highest level of speech, parents and school teams can ask, “Where was communication last successful?” and restart from there.

This might mean returning to pointing, choosing, whispering, speaking to a parent near the teacher, or answering yes/no questions before expecting full speech again. The goal is to rebuild momentum through successful steps.

What This Means For Parents

If your child stops speaking again after making progress, try not to panic. A setback does not erase previous progress. It usually means the current demand is too high for the child’s anxiety level right now.

The best response is often to temporarily make communication easier. If your child was speaking in phrases but now freezes, return to one-word responses or forced-choice questions. If one-word responses are too hard, return to nodding, pointing, or whispering. This is not going backward permanently. It is using an easier step to rebuild safety.

A useful strategy is to create a “reset ladder.” Write down the communication steps your child has already achieved, from easiest to hardest. During a setback, restart at the easiest step that still works, then slowly move upward again.

You can also look for what changed. Did school restart? Did the seating chart change? Did a teacher leave? Did a peer comment on your child’s speech? Did routines become less predictable? Understanding the trigger can help you adjust the plan.

Parents often accidentally respond to regression by increasing reminders, encouragement, pressure, and attention on speaking. A more effective response is usually calmer: reduce pressure, restore predictability, create small wins, and rebuild confidence.

What To Avoid

  • Avoid saying, “But you were talking last week.”
  • Avoid treating setbacks as proof that progress was fake.
  • Avoid immediately pushing your child back to their highest previous speaking level.
  • Avoid showing panic or disappointment when speech decreases.
  • Avoid stopping all practice completely unless your child is overwhelmed.
  • Avoid assuming a setback means treatment or support failed.
  • Avoid ignoring possible triggers like routine changes, peer comments, illness, or school transitions.
