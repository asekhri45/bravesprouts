Quick Answer

Tell relatives that selective mutism is an anxiety disorder, not rudeness, stubbornness, disrespect, or a child “choosing not to talk.” Your child may be able to speak comfortably in some places and still be unable to speak with certain relatives because different people and settings create different levels of anxiety.

The most helpful relatives are usually the ones who reduce pressure, stay warm, avoid putting the child on the spot, and allow communication to build gradually. A relative does not need to “get the child to talk” to be supportive. Often, the best first goal is helping the child feel safe enough to participate in any way they can.

Research Explanation

Selective mutism is often misunderstood by extended family because the child may speak normally at home but become silent at family gatherings, holidays, visits, restaurants, or unfamiliar houses. This can make relatives assume the child is being rude, spoiled, defiant, or overly dependent on parents.

That misunderstanding can create more pressure. A grandparent might say, “Come on, say hi,” an aunt might ask repeated questions, or a cousin might ask, “Why don’t you talk?” These reactions are usually well-intended, but they can make the child feel watched and evaluated.

ASHA explains that children with selective mutism are often anxious about communication demands and may use avoidance as a form of self-protection. These behaviors can be mistaken for being oppositional, difficult, or rude. That distinction is important because relatives respond very differently when they understand the silence as anxiety rather than disrespect. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

The NHS similarly describes selective mutism as an anxiety disorder where a child or adult is unable to speak in certain social situations, including with relatives they do not see often. It also emphasizes that the person is not refusing or choosing not to speak; they are unable to speak in those situations. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

A useful way to explain this to family is: “Speech is available for our child in some settings, but not yet in others.” This avoids making the conversation about manners or personality. It also helps relatives understand that the goal is to expand communication gradually, not demand speech immediately.

Relatives can help by becoming low-pressure people. This means greeting warmly without requiring a verbal greeting, commenting instead of questioning, allowing pointing or nodding, giving time, and focusing on shared activities rather than speech. For example, a relative can say, “I’m happy to see you,” and then continue naturally instead of waiting for the child to answer.

Selective mutism interventions often use gradual exposure, shaping, reinforcement, and stimulus fading. These principles can apply to relatives too. A child may first tolerate being near a relative, then play beside them, then point to something, then whisper to a parent nearby, then answer a forced-choice question, and eventually speak more directly.

A major insight for relatives is that connection comes before speech. A child may need many neutral, pressure-free experiences with a relative before speech becomes possible. If every interaction begins with “Say hi” or “Why won’t you talk to me?” the relative becomes associated with pressure. If interactions feel calm and predictable, the relative may gradually become part of the child’s circle of safety.

What This Means For Parents

Parents often feel caught between protecting their child and managing relatives’ feelings. It can help to explain selective mutism before family events instead of during a stressful moment. A short message beforehand can prevent relatives from taking the silence personally.

You might say: “We’re working on making communication feel easier for her. Please don’t ask her to say hi or answer questions right away. You can greet her warmly, give her time, and let her join through pointing, smiling, showing, or playing.”

You can also give relatives a specific role. Instead of saying, “Don’t pressure her,” tell them what to do instead: sit nearby, comment on the activity, offer two choices, avoid staring while waiting, and move on if she does not respond.

For relatives who want to help more actively, use a bridge plan. The child might first talk to a parent while the relative is nearby, then whisper to the parent while the relative listens, then answer a yes/no or choice question, then gradually speak more directly. This is much more effective than expecting direct speech at a holiday dinner.

The best family support feels calm, ordinary, and non-dramatic. When relatives stop treating speech as the price of politeness, children often have more room to feel safe.

What To Avoid

  • Avoid letting relatives pressure your child to say hello, thank you, or answer questions on command.
  • Avoid allowing teasing, bribing, guilt, or comments like “You talk at home, so talk here.”
  • Avoid framing silence as disrespect.
  • Avoid forcing hugs, greetings, performances, or public answers.
  • Avoid discussing your child’s mutism in front of them as if they are not listening.
  • Avoid making family gatherings the first place you practice difficult speech goals.
