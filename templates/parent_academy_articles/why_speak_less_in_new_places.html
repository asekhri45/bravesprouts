Quick Answer

Children with selective mutism often speak less in new places because unfamiliar environments increase uncertainty. Even if your child can speak at home, school, or with certain trusted people, a new setting may remove the safety cues their nervous system relies on. New places often include unfamiliar adults, new rules, different sounds, new expectations, and unpredictable social interactions.

This does not mean your child has “gone backward” or is choosing not to speak. It usually means the environment has become more demanding. For many children with selective mutism, speech is not only about ability. It depends on how safe, familiar, and predictable the situation feels.

Research Explanation

Selective mutism is highly context-dependent. ASHA describes selective mutism as affecting communication across people, places, and activities. This means a child’s ability to communicate can change depending on where they are, who is present, and what is expected of them.

New places are difficult because they remove predictability. A child may not know who will talk to them, what questions they might be asked, where they are supposed to stand, what the rules are, or how others will react if they speak. For an anxious nervous system, uncertainty can increase threat perception.

A child may speak comfortably at home because the environment is familiar. They know the rooms, routines, people, voices, and expectations. A new place does not provide the same safety signals. Even small changes, such as a different classroom, new waiting room, unfamiliar store, restaurant, camp, doctor’s office, or sports facility, can raise anxiety enough to make speech harder.

This is one reason children with selective mutism may speak less during transitions. A child who was beginning to speak in one classroom may become quieter after changing teachers. A child who speaks to a coach at one location may become silent at a tournament. A child who talks at home may stop speaking when relatives visit or when the family enters an unfamiliar public setting.

The important insight is that the child may not have lost the skill. They may have lost access to the skill because the context changed. Speech that is available in one setting may not automatically generalize to another setting.

This is why treatment often focuses on generalization. Generalization means helping a child use communication skills across more people, places, and activities. In selective mutism, this usually has to be built deliberately. A child may need repeated successful experiences in slightly different environments before speech begins to feel safe across settings.

Evidence-informed interventions often use gradual exposure, shaping, and stimulus fading. Instead of expecting a child to speak normally in a brand-new place, parents and professionals build smaller steps. The child may first visit the place with no speaking expectation, then communicate nonverbally, then whisper to a parent, then answer a forced-choice question, then speak to a familiar adult, and eventually speak more independently.

A helpful concept for parents is “previewing.” Previewing gives the child information before entering a new setting. This can reduce uncertainty. Pictures, short visits, role-play, maps, simple explanations, or practicing what might happen can help the nervous system feel less surprised.

The goal is not to avoid new places forever. The goal is to make new places less neurologically “new” before expecting speech.

What This Means For Parents

If your child speaks less in new places, try to see it as a predictable anxiety response rather than a failure. New settings increase the difficulty level. That means your child may need easier communication steps at first.

One useful strategy is to separate “entering the place” from “speaking in the place.” For some children, simply entering a new environment calmly is already a meaningful step. Speech can come later after the environment becomes more familiar.

Before going somewhere new, you can preview the situation. Show photos, explain who will be there, describe what will happen first, and give your child a simple plan. For example: “First we will walk in. Then we will sit near the window. The doctor may say hi. You can wave or nod if talking feels hard.”

Another useful approach is to bring a familiar activity into the new place. Drawing, a book, a small game, or a simple job can reduce pressure because attention shifts away from speech. Children often communicate more easily when the focus is on an activity instead of on their face or voice.

If speech does not happen right away, look for smaller forms of communication: pointing, nodding, choosing, showing, writing, whispering, or handing something to another person. These can become stepping stones toward speech.

What To Avoid

  • Avoid expecting your child to speak normally just because they spoke somewhere else.
  • Avoid saying, “You talked at school, so why not here?”
  • Avoid making the first visit to a new place a speaking test.
  • Avoid introducing too many new people and speech demands at once.
  • Avoid treating quietness in new places as regression or defiance.
  • Avoid skipping preparation if your child struggles with transitions.
