Quick Answer

Some children with selective mutism can speak to certain people but not others because speech becomes linked to feelings of safety, familiarity, predictability, and past experiences. This does not mean your child loves one person more than another, trusts one person completely, or is trying to be rude to someone else. It often means their nervous system has learned that speaking feels safer with one person and riskier with another.

Parents sometimes assume, “My child talks to Grandma but not Grandpa, so they must like Grandma more.” In many cases, that is not accurate. A child may feel warmly toward both people, but speech may already be established with one person and not yet established with the other. Once a relationship becomes a “silent relationship,” it can stay that way unless speech is gradually and carefully introduced.

Research Explanation

Selective mutism is situation-specific. Children with selective mutism usually have the physical and language ability to speak, but anxiety interferes with speech in certain social situations. This is why a child may talk freely with a parent, sibling, or close friend but become silent with a relative, neighbor, coach, doctor, teacher, or peer.

One helpful way to understand this is through the idea of “speech safety.” This is not a formal diagnosis, but it is a useful parent-friendly way to describe what often happens. Children may develop small circles of people with whom speech feels safe. Outside of those circles, speech may feel exposed, uncertain, or threatening.

Clinicians sometimes use principles such as stimulus fading to help children expand these circles. In stimulus fading, the child begins communicating with a safe person and then another person is gradually introduced in a low-pressure way. The goal is not to force speech with a new person suddenly. The goal is to help the child’s nervous system learn that speech can remain safe even as the social situation changes.

This matters because selective mutism is not only about the child’s personality. It is also about learning history. If a child has spoken to one person many times in relaxed situations, the brain has evidence that speaking with that person is safe. If the child has never spoken to another person, or if past attempts felt pressured or embarrassing, the brain may treat that person as a higher-risk speaking situation.

Avoidance learning can strengthen this pattern. If a child stays silent around a certain person and the uncomfortable moment passes, the child feels relief. That relief teaches the brain that silence worked. Over time, silence with that person can become automatic, even if the child actually wants to talk.

This explains why selective mutism can look inconsistent. A child might speak to one cousin but not another, one grandparent but not another, one neighbor but not another, or one classmate but not another. The pattern may seem random from the outside, but it often reflects subtle differences in familiarity, pressure, audience size, past experiences, tone of voice, wait time, and predictability.

A major insight for parents is that comfort does not always transfer automatically. Just because a child talks to one adult does not mean they can immediately talk to a similar adult. The child’s brain may not generalize safety that quickly. Generalization often needs to be built step by step.

What This Means For Parents

Try not to interpret your child’s silence with certain people as dislike. A child can love someone deeply and still be unable to speak to them. This is especially important with relatives, who may feel hurt or rejected. You can explain that your child’s speech is affected by anxiety and comfort, not by affection.

A helpful strategy is to use a “bridge person.” Instead of expecting your child to suddenly speak to someone they have never spoken to, start with someone they already speak to comfortably. Then gradually bring the new person closer into the interaction.

For example, your child might first talk to you while Grandpa is in another room. Later, your child might talk to you while Grandpa is nearby but not participating. Then Grandpa might quietly join the activity without asking questions. Eventually, your child might answer a forced-choice question with you still present. This kind of slow progression is much more aligned with anxiety learning than sudden pressure.

Another useful approach is to build speech through activities instead of direct questioning. Children often feel less pressure when attention is on a game, puzzle, drawing, pet, toy, or shared task. The goal is to make communication feel useful and natural, not like a performance.

The most important idea is that speech should be expanded, not demanded. When a child learns, “I can speak near this person and nothing bad happens,” the circle of safe people can slowly grow.

What To Avoid

  • Avoid saying, “You talk to Grandma, so why won’t you talk to Grandpa?”
  • Avoid assuming silence means your child dislikes someone.
  • Avoid asking relatives to pressure, tease, bribe, or trick your child into speaking.
  • Avoid putting your child on the spot during greetings or family gatherings.
  • Avoid expecting comfort with one person to automatically transfer to another person.
  • Avoid treating speech as proof of love, respect, or politeness.
