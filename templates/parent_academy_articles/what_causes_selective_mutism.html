Research Explanation

Selective mutism is one of the most misunderstood childhood anxiety disorders because it can appear, on the surface, as if a child is choosing not to speak.

Parents frequently hear comments such as “She’s just shy,” “He’ll grow out of it,” “She’s being stubborn,” or “He talks at home, so clearly he can talk.” The problem is that these explanations do not match what researchers and clinicians have observed.

Modern research views selective mutism as an anxiety-related condition in which a child’s ability to speak becomes disrupted in specific social situations. The DSM-5-TR classifies selective mutism as an anxiety disorder rather than a behavioral or oppositional disorder. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association also describes selective mutism as a condition that affects communication across specific people, places, and activities.

One of the strongest risk factors researchers have identified is a temperament called behavioral inhibition.

Behaviorally inhibited children tend to observe before participating, become cautious around unfamiliar people, need more time to warm up, react strongly to new environments, and feel overwhelmed more easily by social uncertainty. Parents often notice these traits early in life. A child may hide behind a parent when meeting new people, avoid group activities, become distressed by unexpected changes, or freeze when attention suddenly turns toward them.

Behavioral inhibition is not a disorder by itself. Many inhibited children develop into confident, healthy adults. But when this temperament combines with heightened anxiety, certain social and communication situations can become especially difficult.

Researchers have also found strong connections between selective mutism and social anxiety. Many children with selective mutism show fears related to negative evaluation, embarrassment, mistakes, being judged, or becoming the center of attention. For some children, speaking itself begins to feel threatening because speech creates visibility. Once a child speaks, other people may look, respond, ask follow-up questions, or expect more speech.

This is where avoidance learning becomes important.

Imagine a child is asked a question in class. Their anxiety rises. Their heart races. Their mind goes blank. They remain silent. The teacher moves on. Immediately, the child feels relief.

From the brain’s perspective, silence worked. The child was scared, stayed silent, and escaped the uncomfortable moment. Over dozens or hundreds of similar experiences, this pattern can become deeply reinforced. The child may want to speak, but the nervous system has learned that silence is safer.

This does not mean the child is trying to control the situation. It means their brain has learned a protective response. Unfortunately, that protective response can start to block communication in places where communication is important, such as school, activities, appointments, stores, or social gatherings.

Researchers now generally understand selective mutism as the result of several interacting factors rather than one single cause. These factors can include temperament, inherited vulnerability to anxiety, social anxiety, communication demands, environmental stressors, and avoidance learning.

There is rarely one simple origin story. For many children, selective mutism develops gradually as anxiety and avoidance patterns become stronger over time.

What This Means For Parents

One of the most helpful shifts a parent can make is moving from “My child won’t speak” to “My child can’t comfortably access speech in this moment.”

That distinction changes everything.

When silence is viewed as defiance, the natural response is pressure. Adults may beg, bribe, criticize, punish, or repeatedly ask the child to “just say it.” But if silence is understood as an anxiety response, the response becomes different. The goal becomes support, patience, and gradual skill-building.

Parents often spend years trying to identify the exact thing that caused their child’s selective mutism. They may wonder whether it was preschool, a move, COVID, a bad teacher, a difficult social experience, or something they did as parents.

Specific experiences can sometimes contribute to anxiety, but most children with selective mutism do not have one single clear cause. The condition usually develops from a combination of vulnerabilities and experiences.

That means the most useful question is often not “What caused this?” but “What conditions help my child feel safe enough to communicate?”

For many children, communication becomes easier when demands are smaller, expectations are predictable, adults stay calm, and the child has repeated experiences of communicating successfully without pressure.

Most children with selective mutism want to communicate. Many speak freely at home, laugh with siblings, sing while playing, or talk comfortably with trusted people. That can make the silence in other settings confusing. But the inconsistency is actually part of the disorder. The child’s ability to speak changes depending on the setting, the people present, the level of attention, and the perceived pressure.

Understanding this can help parents become more compassionate and more strategic. Instead of trying to force speech, parents can focus on building small, successful steps toward communication.

What To Avoid

Avoid assuming your child is choosing silence on purpose.

Avoid describing your child as stubborn, rude, lazy, manipulative, oppositional, or disrespectful. These labels can increase shame and make communication feel even harder.

Avoid repeatedly saying “I know you can do it.” While this usually comes from encouragement, it can feel frustrating to a child who already knows they can speak in some situations but cannot access speech in the moment.

Avoid treating selective mutism as something your child will automatically grow out of without support. Some children do improve over time, but untreated anxiety patterns can also become more deeply practiced.

Avoid searching endlessly for one thing to blame. Most parents did not cause their child’s selective mutism. The goal is not blame. The goal is understanding what helps your child move forward.

Most importantly, avoid using pressure as the main strategy. Anxiety rarely improves through force. Progress is usually built through safety, confidence, gradual exposure, and repeated successful communication experiences.
