Quick Answer

Speaking can become harder around other children because peers add a different kind of social pressure. Adults may feel predictable, patient, or easier to read, while children can feel more spontaneous, observant, judgmental, or unpredictable. For a child with selective mutism, speaking around peers may feel like stepping into a spotlight.

This does not mean your child dislikes other children or does not want friends. Many children with selective mutism want social connection very badly. The difficulty is that peer attention can increase anxiety, self-consciousness, and fear of being noticed. As that anxiety rises, speech can become harder to access.

Research Explanation

Selective mutism is closely connected to social anxiety. Social anxiety is not only fear of strangers or adults. It often involves fear of being watched, judged, embarrassed, laughed at, or negatively evaluated. Peer situations can activate these fears intensely because children are often highly sensitive to what other children think.

Parents sometimes assume adults are the hardest audience. In many cases, peers are harder. Adults usually follow social scripts: they ask a question, wait, reassure, or move on. Children may stare, interrupt, laugh, repeat what happened, ask blunt questions, or draw attention to silence without meaning harm. For a child with selective mutism, that unpredictability can feel risky.

Peer settings also increase self-focused attention. A child may begin monitoring everything: “What if my voice sounds weird? What if they laugh? What if they ask why I never talk? What if everyone notices?” This self-monitoring uses mental resources that would otherwise support communication.

Speaking itself becomes more complicated in groups. A one-on-one interaction with a trusted adult has fewer moving parts. A group of children adds turn-taking, speed, facial expressions, jokes, interruptions, competition, and shifting attention. This creates a higher social-cognitive load.

For children with selective mutism, that load can push the nervous system toward freezing. The child may know what they want to say but become unable to enter the conversation quickly enough. By the time they think of a response, the group may have moved on.

Avoidance learning can then reinforce the pattern. If staying silent prevents attention or embarrassment, silence brings relief. Over time, the child may participate less and less, even if they want to join. This can affect friendships because peers may misread silence as disinterest.

A major insight for parents is that peer difficulty is not just about bravery. It is about complexity. Speaking to one calm adult is one level of difficulty. Speaking to one kind peer is harder. Speaking to two peers is harder still. Speaking in a fast-moving group can be dramatically harder.

This is why many evidence-aligned strategies build peer communication gradually. Instead of moving from silence to full group participation, children often benefit from a sequence: one safe peer, then two peers, then a small predictable group, then larger groups.

What This Means For Parents

If your child struggles more around other children, do not assume they are antisocial. They may want connection but feel blocked by anxiety. Some children with selective mutism observe play closely, smile, follow along, or stay near peers even when they cannot speak. These can be signs of social interest.

A helpful strategy is to start with one carefully chosen peer. The best first peer is not always the child’s favorite classmate. It is often the calmest, kindest, least pressuring child. A predictable peer can become a bridge into social communication.

Structured activities are often easier than open-ended conversation. Building blocks, drawing, board games, scavenger hunts, crafts, sorting games, or simple shared tasks reduce pressure because the focus is on the activity rather than on speaking.

Parents can also help by arranging short, low-pressure interactions instead of long playdates. A 20-minute successful interaction with one child may be more useful than a two-hour overwhelming playdate. The goal is repeated success, not maximum exposure.

Once one peer feels safer, the circle can expand slowly. The child might first communicate nonverbally, then whisper, then use one-word responses, then short phrases. Each step teaches the brain that peer communication can be safe.

What To Avoid

  • Avoid assuming your child does not want friends.
  • Avoid pushing your child directly into large groups before one-on-one peer comfort develops.
  • Avoid telling peers, “Try to make them talk.”
  • Avoid long, unstructured playdates if your child becomes overwhelmed.
  • Avoid praising speech so loudly that other children notice and stare.
  • Avoid treating peer silence as rudeness or lack of social interest.
